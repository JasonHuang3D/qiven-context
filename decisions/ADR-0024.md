# Prefer correct downward semantic ownership over local duplication or staged refactoring

## Context

The earlier Foundation rule correctly resisted speculative utility growth, but its practical interpretation drifted toward a "product-local first, extract later" default. During `qiven-dcr-win` Batch 001 this encouraged writing process/runtime behavior in the product before fully evaluating lower-layer ownership, followed by later attempts to add Foundation conventions and refactor the implementation.

That sequence recreates a common human development history: local shortcut, duplicated lower semantics, debt, then extraction. In an AI-led engineering system with broad prior knowledge, reproducing that learning curve is unnecessary when semantic placement is already knowable.

## Decision

Dependencies continue to point downward, but a correct downward dependency is considered beneficial rather than a cost to avoid.

Before implementing a capability in layer `A`, determine its natural semantic owner. When an existing lower layer `B` is the clear owner and can express the contract without absorbing higher-level policy, implement or refine the capability in `B` first and consume it from `A`.

Multiple downstream consumers are evidence of reuse but are not a prerequisite for correct placement. One concrete downstream requirement may justify a lower-layer addition when the abstraction is intrinsically lower-level, stable enough to specify, and compatible with the lower layer's ownership, failure, dependency, portability/platform-boundary, and performance laws.

Foundation remains small and product-free, but "small" means semantically disciplined rather than intentionally incomplete. If a needed capability is below the product but above Foundation, create or refine the appropriate shared semantic layer such as Runtime or Networking rather than duplicating it locally or forcing it into Foundation.

The first serious implementation should aim directly at candidate-quality placement and contracts. Local prototypes are reserved for cases where the semantic contract itself is genuinely unknown; such prototypes are disposable evidence-gathering artifacts, not a default staging step.

## Consequences

Product batches may legitimately pause to improve a lower dependency when the product exposes a real missing primitive. Such work is part of the product objective, not automatically infrastructure scope creep.

Cross-repository work should normally validate the lower-layer change first and bind the consumer to an exact accepted integration baseline before finalizing the upper layer.

Review should treat duplicated lower-layer behavior, cosmetic dependency avoidance, and "we can refactor later" as design smells when the correct owner is already known.

Performance, ownership, bounded resources, concurrency, and failure semantics are considered at the lower-layer design point rather than deferred until product integration.

## Relationship to ADR-0007

This ADR supersedes ADR-0007 while preserving its important constraints: Foundation remains foundational, low-dependency, predictable, explicit, and free of product/domain semantics. The superseded part is the implication that a primitive should normally remain in higher layers until repeated or multi-consumer demand appears. Semantic ownership now outranks consumer count.

## Alternatives Considered

Product-local implementation first with later extraction; avoiding shared dependencies to preserve repository independence; requiring two or more consumers before adding any lower-layer primitive; broad speculative completion of Foundation or Runtime before product work.

## Revisit Conditions

Revisit only if this rule causes lower layers to absorb unstable higher-level policy, produces measurable coordination cost greater than the duplication it prevents, or if a better formal criterion for semantic placement can preserve direct first-pass quality with less architectural coupling.

## Provenance

Accepted after the project owner identified repeated rework in `qiven-dcr-win` as an avoidable consequence of imitating human trial-and-refactor development rather than applying known engineering architecture directly.
